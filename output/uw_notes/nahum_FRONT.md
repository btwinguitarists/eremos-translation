# Introduction to Nahum

## General Introduction

### Outline of Nahum

1. Nahum introduces this book ([1:1](../01/01.md))
1. Yahweh will destroy his enemies ([1:2–6](../01/02.md))
1. Yahweh cares for his people and will destroy Nineveh ([1:7–15](../01/07.md))
1. Nahum describes how Nineveh will fall ([2:1–3:19](../02/01.md))

### What is the book of Nahum about?

The book of Nahum contains prophecies about how Yahweh would judge and punish Nineveh.

Nineveh was the capital city of the Assyrian Empire. The Assyrian Empire was very powerful and cruel. It was conquering and absorbing the kingdoms all around it. The Assyrians had already conquered the northern kingdom of Israel. They were threatening the southern kingdom of Judah. The audience of the book is the people of Judah. This book was to encourage their faith in God and to give them confidence that the Assyrians would be defeated.

### How should the title of this book be translated?

“The Book of Nahum” or just “Nahum” is the traditional title of this book. Translators may choose to call it “The Prophecies of Nahum.” (See: [[rc://*/ta/man/translate/translate-names]])

### Who wrote the book of Nahum?

The prophet Nahum spoke these prophecies, and either he or one of his disciples wrote them down. Nahum was a prophet from Elkosh, an unknown city, probably in Judah.

Nahum mentions the destruction of Thebes, a city in Egypt, as a past event, which happened about 663 B.C. He prophesied about the destruction of Nineveh, which happened in 612 B.C. Therefore, the book of Nahum was written sometime between 663 and 612 B.C. (See: [[rc://*/tw/dict/bible/kt/prophet]])

## Translation Issues

### Personification

In this prophecy, Yahweh often addresses the people of Judah and the people of Nineveh as simply “Judah” and “Nineveh,” using the names of the nation and city to represent the people. He also uses verbs in the singular that describe the nation or city doing things that people do, such as Judah wearing a yoke and shackles [1:13](../01/13.md) or celebrating festivals and fulfilling vows [1:15](../01/15.md). He describes Nineveh as a soldier who guards walls, watches a road, and strengthens his loins [2:1](../02/01.md), and also as a prostitute whom Yahweh will strip naked and cover with filth [3:4–6](../03/04.md), and as a drunkard who hides himself [3:11](../03/11.md). If it is not clear that these things describe the people as a whole, you could explain this in a footnote or use one of the ideas in [personification](rc://*/ta/man/translate/figs-personification).

### Short, unconnected statements

In the descriptions of the battle for Nineveh, Nahum uses short statements that are not connected to the rest of the prophecy. For example, it is difficult to tell who “He” and “they” are in [2:5](../02/05.md) or who is saying “Stop, stop” in [2:8](../02/08.md). There is no explanation of who the statements of [2:10](../02/10.md) apply to, “Empty and desolate and ruined! And the heart is melted, and a shaking of knees, and anguish {is} in all loins, and all of their faces gather beauty.” The statements of [3:1–3](../03/01.md) are similar. The reason seems to be that Nahum is reproducing in his description the confusion and anguish and the overwhelming of the senses that occurs in battle. Try to reproduce this effect in your translation in a way that makes sense in your language.
