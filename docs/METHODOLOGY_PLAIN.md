# How the Eremos Translation was made — in plain language

The same story as [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md), told without the
technical vocabulary. Written for conversations — with a pastor, a supporter,
a curious friend, or anyone weighing what this project is.

---

## The one-minute version

The Eremos Translation is a complete Thai Bible — all 66 books — translated
from the original Hebrew and Greek, not from English. It was drafted by an AI
working under strict written rules, checked by layers of automatic tests and
by rival AI systems, and every single verse keeps a written record of *why* it
was translated the way it was. It's been given away completely — public
domain, free forever, for anyone, including for print, including commercially.

What it is **not**, yet: reviewed line-by-line by a body of native Thai
readers. That's the current phase. The honest way to say it: the fast part is
done and thoroughly documented; the slow, human part is beginning, and the
first Thai reviewers are being recruited right now. No book gets called
finished until Thai readers have been through it.

## What the AI did — and what it couldn't do

The AI did the drafting: it read the Greek or Hebrew of each chapter — with
word-by-word grammatical data scholars use — plus background notes from a
respected open scholarship project, and wrote Thai. For every verse it also
wrote down its reasoning: which words were hard, which had more than one
possible meaning, where ancient manuscripts differ, and why it chose what it
chose. Think of a translator who shows *all* their work, on every verse, and
never gets tired.

What it deliberately never did: look at anyone else's Thai Bible while
translating. Not the copyrighted ones, not even the free one. That rule
exists for two reasons — legally, it means our text can't have absorbed
anyone else's copyrighted wording, which is what lets us give it away; and
honestly, it means the translation is a genuinely independent reading of the
original languages, not a remix of existing translations.

And what it *can't* do: know how a verse lands in a Thai heart. Whether it
sounds like something a real person would say. That's not a machine's
judgment to make — which is why the review phase belongs to Thai readers.

## The safety nets

A first draft — anyone's first draft — can't be trusted without checks. So
the pipeline refuses to accept a chapter until it passes a battery of them:

- **Translate it back.** Every chapter gets translated literally back into
  English and compared against a trusted English translation of the same
  Greek/Hebrew. Places where they disagree have to be explained or fixed.
- **Same word, same way.** A running glossary — over nineteen thousand
  entries — tracks how every important word has been rendered, so "grace"
  doesn't quietly become three different Thai words across three books. If a
  word is translated differently somewhere, there has to be a written reason.
- **Show your receipts.** If a verse note claims something ("this quotes
  Isaiah"), a checker verifies the claim was actually recorded in the
  project's databases. Claims without receipts stop the ship.
- **Second opinions from rivals.** When a book finishes, its hardest
  decisions are put to competing AI systems from other companies —
  deliberately not the one that did the translating, because a translator
  checking their own work has the same blind spots twice. Their objections
  are then verified word-by-word against the actual text (they sometimes
  invent things) and the real issues get dealt with.
- **The whole-Bible sweep.** After all 66 books, a final audit looked across
  books for the things no single-book check can see — like the same Old
  Testament verse being quoted slightly differently in the New Testament.
  The remaining cross-Bible consistency questions are on a public worklist
  being decided one by one.

One more thing worth saying plainly: some of these safety nets exist because
something once got through. Each time a mistake was caught, a permanent
automatic check was built so that *kind* of mistake can't happen again — and
the incident reports are public in the project's history. That's not a
weakness of the method; that's the method.

## Where trust comes from

Most translations ask you to trust the committee. This one asks you to check
the work — because all of it is public: every rule the translator followed,
every verse's reasoning, every mistake caught and fixed, every decision
document. A scholar can audit any verse back to the Greek or Hebrew. A Thai
reader can flag any sentence that stumbles, and there's a form built for
exactly that. Openness is the accountability model.

## Why give it away?

Thailand has good Thai Bibles, and this project isn't against any of them —
it complements them. But the good ones are copyrighted, which limits what
believers and builders can do: print freely, quote at any length, build apps,
make audio, adapt for teaching. A public-domain Bible means Thai Christians
own their own Scripture infrastructure. The Eremos app and its tools are the
first proof of what that unlocks; they won't be the last.

## Questions people actually ask — and honest answers

**"So a computer translated the Bible?"**
A computer *drafted* it — from the original languages, under written rules,
showing its reasoning on every verse, checked by layers of tests and by rival
systems. Human judgment set the rules, audits every decision, and Thai
readers give the final verdict. Nobody's calling it finished until they do.

**"How do you know it's accurate?"**
Three layers. Every verse was translated directly from the Hebrew/Greek with
scholarly grammatical data. Every chapter had to survive back-translation
comparison and consistency checks before it could ship. And every book was
cross-examined by independent AI reviewers, with every claim verified against
the text. What remains — naturalness in Thai ears — is exactly what the
review phase is for.

**"Who has actually reviewed it?"**
Machines and rival AIs, thoroughly. Thai people — honestly, only early
readers so far, and their feedback has been encouraging. A purpose-built
review system is live and the first reviewers are being recruited now. That's
the current chapter of the story, and the reason I bring the project up.

**"Isn't this disrespectful to how Bible translation is done?"**
The project runs on the same professional standards it could borrow —
industry-standard translation principles, the same Greek text base as the
major evangelical translations, mainstream policy on disputed verses
(bracketed or footnoted the way ESV/NIV-family Bibles do it). What's new is
the speed of drafting and the depth of the paper trail. The traditional slow
part — native readers weighing every line — isn't being skipped; it's being
done second instead of first, with better documentation to work from.

**"What if there are mistakes in it?"**
There are — somewhere, in thirty-one thousand verses, guaranteed. The claim
isn't perfection; it's that every verse is traceable, every correction is
cheap and public, and the license means a fixed verse reaches every reader's
app quickly instead of waiting for a reprint.

**"Why should Thailand's Bible come from a foreigner and a machine?"**
It shouldn't — and this isn't that. It's a draft and an open toolset, given
away so that Thai believers can make it theirs: review it, correct it, own
it, build on it. The whole design — open license, open process, review system
in Thai — exists to put Thai readers in the driver's seat of their own text.
